# Scientific Basis for Longevity Daily Advisor

Synthesized from top-tier papers on mitochondria, exercise, diet, aging, and the New Scientist article "Your body changes as you age and so should your diet – here’s how" (27 July 2026).

## Core Principles

### Mitochondrial Health as Central Hub
- Mitochondria decline with age: reduced biogenesis, impaired quality control (fusion/fission, mitophagy), higher ROS, mtDNA damage.
- Exercise is the strongest evidence-based intervention to restore mitochondrial content, energetics, and quality control (AMPK → PGC-1α, SIRT1, mitophagy pathways).
- Dietary strategies (moderate energy restriction patterns, nutrient density, anti-inflammatory foods) support the same pathways via AMPK, SIRT, mTOR modulation, and reduced inflammation.

### Age-Related Physiological Changes (from New Scientist + literature)
- From ~40 onwards: declining mitochondrial efficiency, reduced nutrient absorption, easier muscle loss (sarcopenia), increased visceral fat and chronic low-grade inflammation.
- From ~50–60: appetite and metabolism decline; higher relative need for protein, fiber, calcium, vitamin D, magnesium, B vitamins, omega-3s per calorie.
- Protein needs rise (aim 1.2–1.6 g/kg for older adults, especially with resistance training) while total calories often fall → nutrient density is critical.
- Midlife dietary patterns strongly predict healthspan at 70+; changes even after 60 still yield benefits (modeled 6–10 year gains possible with better patterns).

### Exercise Evidence Hierarchy
1. **Combined aerobic + resistance** is optimal.
2. Aerobic / Zone 2 (moderate intensity, conversational pace, ~50–70% HRmax): primary driver of mitochondrial biogenesis and volume.
3. Resistance training (2–3×/week, major muscle groups, near failure 6–12 reps): improves mitochondrial quality, muscle mass/strength, mtDNA health, functional capacity.
4. Occasional HIIT can efficiently stimulate adaptations.
5. Consistency over years matters more than perfection. Lifelong activity preserves mitochondrial function far better than sedentary aging.

### Dietary Patterns for Healthy Aging
- Prioritize: fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, healthy fats (olive oil, fatty fish), adequate high-quality protein.
- Limit: sugary drinks, ultra-processed foods, excess red/processed meat, excess saturated fat.
- Emphasize fiber (whole grains especially), magnesium, zinc, selenium, omega-3s, calcium + vitamin D.
- Nutrient density becomes more important with age because volume of food often decreases.
- Time-of-day considerations are secondary to overall pattern, but practical meal structure helps adherence and energy.

### Key Actionable Targets
- Protein: distribute across meals; higher absolute intake as age increases or with training.
- Fiber: aim high via whole grains, legumes, vegetables.
- Anti-inflammatory: omega-3 rich fish, colorful plants, nuts.
- Micronutrients of rising importance: Ca, Vit D, Mg, B vitamins, selenium (e.g., Brazil nuts).
- Energy balance: avoid chronic surplus; mild restriction patterns or time-restricted eating can support mitochondrial quality when combined with resistance training to protect muscle.

## Practical Translation Rules
- Always prioritize whole-food, high-nutrient-density choices.
- Adjust recommendations by time of day for practicality and energy needs (higher protein earlier if training; lighter evening if desired).
- For exercise plans: include both aerobic (volume for mitochondria) and resistance (quality + muscle preservation). Progress gradually. Include recovery.
- Individualize based on user-provided age, activity level, preferences, constraints, and any health conditions (advise professional consultation when needed).
- Never recommend extreme restriction that risks muscle loss or nutrient deficiency in older adults.
